## The IRS has a new streamlined way to report tax fraud

The IRS recently simplified the process for reporting suspected tax fraud, replacing a patchwork of forms and mailing addresses with a more direct submission path. For a Westport CT CPA firm working with small business owners across Fairfield County and the tri-state area, this matters because clients are asking questions faster than the IRS is answering them.

The update covers fraud involving preparers, employers, and business partners, and it's designed to get reports in front of IRS Criminal Investigation and civil enforcement teams more efficiently. Whether you've spotted a discrepancy in a partner's filing or you're worried about a preparer who seems to be cutting corners, knowing the right channel, and the right way to protect yourself while using it, is the first step. That's where a conversation with your CPA should come before any form gets submitted.

<!-- block: checklist-section | variant: with-image | image: tax-fraud-reporting-review.jpg | alt: "Accountant reviewing tax documents with a client at a desk" | query: "accountant reviewing tax documents client" -->
## Who should use this reporting process

This reporting path isn't for every tax disagreement. It's built for specific situations where someone else's conduct puts your finances, or your business, at risk.

- You suspect a tax preparer misrepresented deductions or filed a return you never approved
- A business partner or co-owner is underreporting income on a jointly filed entity return
- An employer misclassified you or withheld payroll taxes that never reached the IRS
- A property management company or real estate partner appears to be inflating expenses or hiding rental income
- A vendor or contractor issued a 1099 that doesn't match what you were actually paid

Retail owners, real estate professionals, and professional service providers in Fairfield County run into these scenarios more often than people expect, usually because multiple parties touch the same books.

<!-- block: content-prose -->
## What this means for your own tax compliance

Hearing about a new fraud-reporting tool can make an honest business owner nervous, even when there's nothing to worry about. The reality is straightforward: clean records and consistent filing history are still the best protection you have, whether or not fraud ever touches your business.

The IRS cross-references data from payroll providers, banks, and 1099 filers. If your bookkeeping is current and your entity structure matches how you actually operate, discrepancies get resolved with a phone call instead of an audit letter. Business owners who fall behind on reconciliations or mix personal and business expenses are the ones most likely to get pulled into a fraud investigation involving someone else, simply because their own records raise questions first.

Staying current isn't about fear of the IRS. It's about making sure your name never ends up in the same file as someone else's mistake.

## Frequently Asked Questions About IRS gives taxpayers a streamlined way to report tax fraud

**Q: How do I report suspected tax fraud to the IRS?**
A: The IRS now offers a more direct process for reporting suspected fraud by preparers, employers, or business partners. Before submitting anything, review your own records with a CPA to understand how the situation might affect your filing history and avoid unintended exposure.

**Q: Can I get audited if someone else commits tax fraud against me?**
A: Yes, especially if your records overlap with theirs, such as shared entities, payroll, or 1099 income. Keeping clean, CPA-reviewed bookkeeping is the most reliable way to separate your compliance record from someone else's misconduct.

**Q: What industries face higher tax fraud risk in Connecticut?**
A: Real estate, retail, equine, and influencer businesses often involve multiple third parties handling income and expenses, which increases the chance of reporting errors or fraud. Industry-specific bookkeeping and review help catch red flags before the IRS does.

**Q: Should I talk to a CPA before amending a tax return?**
A: Yes. Amending a return based on suspected fraud involves specific documentation and timing considerations. A CPA familiar with your filing history can help you avoid mistakes that create new compliance issues while you're trying to fix an old one.
