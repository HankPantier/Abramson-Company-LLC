Most insurance advice sounds like a shopping list: get life insurance, get disability insurance, get an umbrella policy, get this, get that. That approach treats insurance like a box to check instead of a math problem to solve. The real question isn't which policies exist. It's how much financial exposure you actually carry, and where a loss would actually hurt.

We see this constantly with clients in Westport and across Fairfield County: a family with a modest income but a $2.5 million home carrying a $300,000 umbrella policy, while a business owner generating $400,000 a year in revenue has no disability coverage at all. The first client is probably fine. The second one is one bad diagnosis away from losing the business. Insurance planning done right starts with your balance sheet and your cash flow, not a generic checklist.

## Insurance is a risk transfer decision, not a purchase decision

Every insurance policy answers one question: what happens if this specific bad thing happens, and can you absorb the cost yourself? If you have $50,000 sitting in reserves and your car gets totaled, you can absorb that. If your rental property in Fairfield County floods and repairs run $180,000, most people cannot absorb that without coverage.

The amount of coverage you need is a function of two numbers: your net worth (what you'd lose) and your cash flow (what you'd need to keep functioning while you recover). A 45-year-old sole proprietor with $1.2 million in assets and $150,000 in annual revenue has a completely different risk profile than a 45-year-old employee with the same net worth and a W-2 salary backed by employer disability coverage. Same asset picture, very different insurance answer.

## Start with what you actually stand to lose

Before buying anything, run the numbers on four categories: your liquid net worth, your illiquid assets (real estate, business equity, retirement accounts with penalties for early withdrawal), your annual cash flow needs, and your dependents' financial runway if your income stopped tomorrow.

A client with $3 million in a diversified portfolio and no dependents needs very different life insurance than a client with $400,000 in savings, a mortgage, and two kids headed to college. The first client is essentially self-insured. The second one has a real gap, and a $1 million term policy costing roughly $600 to $1,200 a year (depending on age and health) closes it far more efficiently than trying to save your way there.

This is the same discipline we bring to [financial planning conversations](/resources/5-tips-to-build-a-healthy-money-mindset-and-grow-your-finances) with clients: know the number before you make the decision. Insurance without a number behind it is guesswork wearing a suit.

## Business owners face a different exposure curve

If you run a business, your personal insurance needs and your business insurance needs are not separate conversations. A real estate agent working across Fairfield County and southern New York carries liability exposure every time they show a property. A retail owner in downtown Westport carries inventory risk, general liability, and workers' comp obligations the moment they hire a single employee. An equine boarding operation carries liability exposure that most general umbrella policies don't even contemplate. A video production company hauling $80,000 in camera equipment to a shoot has an equipment-in-transit exposure that a standard business owner's policy often excludes.

Here's a simple framework for sizing business coverage against actual exposure, not against what a policy checklist tells you to buy:

| Risk category | What it protects against | Ask yourself |
|---|---|---|
| General liability | Third-party injury or property damage claims | What's my highest-traffic client interaction, and what's the worst-case claim? |
| Professional liability (E&O) | Claims of negligent advice or service failure | If a client sued over my work product, what would a settlement realistically cost? |
| Property and equipment | Physical loss of inventory, tools, or gear | What's the replacement cost of everything I'd need to rebuy tomorrow? |
| Business interruption | Lost income while you can't operate | How many months of fixed costs could I cover with cash on hand? |
| Cyber liability | Data breach, ransomware, client data exposure | How many client records do I hold, and what's my state's breach notification cost? |
| Key person / disability | Loss of the person who drives revenue | If I (or my top producer) were out for six months, what's the revenue hit? |

Premiums for most business insurance are deductible as ordinary business expenses, which is worth factoring into the real cost of coverage. The [IRS Publication 535 guidance on business expenses](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p535) covers what qualifies and what doesn't, and it's worth a look before you assume every premium dollar reduces your tax bill the same way.

## Where over-insurance quietly drains cash flow

Over-insuring doesn't feel like a mistake. It feels responsible. But paying $4,800 a year for a $2 million umbrella policy when your total exposed assets are $600,000 isn't caution, it's a cash flow leak. We've reviewed policies for clients paying for coverage limits that exceed their entire net worth by three or four times, coverage stacked on top of coverage they already had through an employer or a professional association, and riders on policies that duplicate what a general liability policy already covers.

The fix isn't to under-insure. It's to size coverage to actual exposure and redirect the difference toward something that builds wealth instead of just protecting against a loss that was never realistically that large. This is the same lens we apply when we help clients [rebalance an investment portfolio](/resources/strategically-balancing-your-investment-portfolio-why-set-it): the goal is matching resources to actual risk, not maximizing coverage for its own sake.

## A simple way to size up your coverage

Run this exercise once a year, ideally when you do your tax planning:

1. List every asset you own and its current value.
2. List every liability and recurring fixed cost.
3. Estimate what a worst-case claim would cost in each risk category above.
4. Compare that number to your current policy limits.
5. Flag any gap larger than $50,000 and any overlap where two policies cover the same exposure.

For business owners in real estate, retail, or professional services, this exercise should happen alongside your annual tax and cash flow review, not as a separate errand. A CPA who already knows your revenue, your entity structure, and your growth plans can spot the mismatch faster than an insurance agent working from a form. ## Common Questions

**How much insurance coverage do I actually need?**
Enough to cover the gap between what you could lose in a worst-case scenario and what you could pay out of pocket without financial strain. That number comes from your net worth, your liquid savings, and your dependents' income needs, not from a generic rule like "ten times your salary."

**What types of insurance matter most for financial protection?**
For most individuals, that's health, disability, life (if you have dependents), homeowners or renters, auto, and an umbrella policy once your net worth exceeds your underlying liability limits. For business owners, add general liability, professional liability if you give advice or services, and property coverage matched to actual replacement cost.

**Can I deduct business insurance premiums on my taxes?**
Most ordinary and necessary business insurance premiums are deductible, as outlined in [IRS Publication 535](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p535). The specifics depend on your entity type and the kind of policy, so it's worth confirming with your CPA before assuming a premium qualifies.

**How often should I review my coverage?**
At least once a year, and any time your net worth, income, or business structure changes materially. A home purchase, a new hire, or a jump in revenue all shift your exposure enough to warrant a fresh look.
