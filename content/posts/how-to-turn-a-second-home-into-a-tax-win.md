Say a client in Westport buys a place in the Litchfield Hills, or maybe a condo in Stratton, Vermont, planning to use it most weekends and rent it out the rest of the time. The first question isn't "how much can I charge?" It's "how many days am I renting this, and how many days am I using it myself?" Those two numbers, tracked on a calendar, determine whether the IRS treats that property as a personal retreat with a nice tax break attached, or a rental business with income to report and losses to justify. Get the days wrong, or don't track them at all, and you either leave deductions on the table or invite a notice from the IRS.

This is the piece most real estate content skips. Realtors talk about square footage and lake frontage. The tax code talks about days.

## The IRS counts days, not intentions

Under [IRS Publication 527](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p527), a home you own is classified based on two counts you track for the calendar year: the number of days you rent it out at a fair market rate, and the number of days you or your family use it personally. "Personal use" isn't limited to you sleeping there. It includes use by your spouse, kids, siblings, parents, or anyone who pays less than fair rental value, plus any day you let a friend stay for free. It also includes days you're there doing repairs only if that's the main purpose of the visit and you're not also using the pool and grilling with the neighbors.

Those two counts, rental days and personal-use days, sort the property into one of three tax buckets. Each bucket has a different answer for what you can deduct and whether a loss can offset your other income.

## The 14-day rule that changes everything

Here's the rule that surprises most people: if you rent the home out for 14 days or fewer during the year, you don't report the rental income at all. Not on Schedule E, not anywhere. It's tax-free, full stop, regardless of how much you charged. This is sometimes called the "Masters exemption" because homeowners near the Masters golf tournament in Augusta rent out their houses for a week at a premium and pocket the income tax-free.

The tradeoff: you also can't deduct rental expenses in that scenario. Mortgage interest and property taxes still flow through as itemized deductions on Schedule A, same as any second home, but you don't get to write off cleaning fees, a property manager's cut, or depreciation. For someone renting a Westport-area or coastal Connecticut property for a single summer week or two, that's usually a fine trade. Tax-free income with no bookkeeping beats a small deduction with a reporting requirement.

## Crossing into rental property territory

Once you rent the home for 15 days or more in a year, the calculus changes. Now you must report the rental income, and you get to deduct rental expenses, but how much you can deduct depends on your personal-use days.

The test, per [IRS Tax Topic 415](https://www.irs.gov/taxtopics/tc415), asks whether your personal use exceeded the greater of 14 days or 10% of the days the home was rented at fair market value. If it did, the IRS treats the property as a personal residence with some rental activity. You can still deduct rental expenses, but only up to the amount of rental income; you can't use the property to generate a paper loss that offsets your salary or investment income. If your personal use falls under that threshold, the home is treated as a rental property, full stop, and losses may be deductible subject to the passive activity loss rules that apply to any rental real estate.

Here's how that breaks down in practice:

| Scenario | Rental days | Personal use days | Tax treatment |
|---|---|---|---|
| Tax-free rental | 14 or fewer | Any | No income reported, no rental deductions, mortgage interest/taxes still itemized |
| Vacation home, heavy personal use | 15 or more | More than the greater of 14 days or 10% of rental days | Report income; expenses deductible only up to rental income (no loss allowed) |
| Rental property, limited personal use | 15 or more | 14 or fewer days, and under 10% of rental days | Report income; losses may be deductible subject to passive activity loss limits |

A client who owns a Nantucket or Cape Cod house, uses it three weeks a summer, and rents it 20 weeks the rest of the year needs to run these numbers before assuming they can deduct a loss. Fourteen days of personal use against 140 rental days is well under the 10% mark, so that property likely qualifies for full rental treatment. Bump personal use to five or six weeks, and the math flips.

## Second home vs investment property: why the label matters beyond taxes

The IRS test above is about deductions. Your lender uses a different, related test to decide your mortgage rate and terms. A "second home" for lending purposes generally means you occupy it part of the year and don't rent it out full-time; an "investment property" is one bought primarily to generate rental income, and it typically carries a higher interest rate and a bigger down payment requirement.

Here's where things get interesting: a property can be a "second home" on your mortgage application and still get taxed as a rental if you cross the 15-day/personal-use threshold in a given year. The two classifications don't have to match, and lenders don't check your tax return every year to see if your rental activity changed. That mismatch is exactly why we tell clients weighing a purchase to model out realistic rental days before closing, not after the first tenant moves in. ## Turning the rules into an actual strategy

Once you know which bucket your property falls into, a few moves can shift the outcome in your favor:

Track every day, in writing. A shared calendar or a simple spreadsheet noting who stayed at the property and whether they paid fair rent is the difference between substantiating a deduction and losing it in an audit. The IRS doesn't take your word for it after the fact.

Time major repairs carefully. Days spent at the property performing substantial repairs or maintenance, where that's the main reason for the trip, don't count as personal-use days even if you sleep there. That distinction can be the difference between staying under the 10% threshold and tipping over it.

Consider intentionally limiting personal use if the rental income and depreciation are worth more to you than a few extra weekends at the property. For a two-family or four-family owned by a Fairfield County real estate investor, this calculation often favors minimizing personal use entirely.

Revisit the numbers every year. Rental demand, your own vacation plans, and family circumstances change year to year, and a property that qualified for full rental treatment in 2023 might not in 2024 if you used it more.

## Common Questions

**Do I have to report rental income if I only rent my second home for a week or two a year?**
No. If you rent the property for 14 days or fewer during the year, the IRS doesn't require you to report that income at all, and you can charge whatever the market allows. You also can't deduct rental-specific expenses for those days, though mortgage interest and property taxes remain deductible as they would be for any second home.

**What's the difference between a second home and an investment property for tax purposes?**
A second home is one you use personally for more than 14 days a year, or more than 10% of the days it's rented, whichever is greater; an investment property is one where personal use stays under that threshold. The label affects whether rental losses can offset your other income, separate from how your mortgage lender classifies the property.

**Can I deduct a loss on my vacation home if I rented it out at a loss this year?**
Only if the property qualifies as a rental under the personal-use test. If your personal use exceeds the greater of 14 days or 10% of rental days, expense deductions are capped at your rental income for the year, meaning no deductible loss. Below that threshold, losses may be deductible subject to passive activity loss rules under [IRS Publication 527](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p527).

**Does the 14-day rule reset every year?**
Yes. The count is based on the calendar year, so a property that qualified as tax-free rental income in one year could cross into reportable rental territory the next if you increase the number of days it's rented out.
