Every few years, the lease-versus-buy conversation resurfaces, usually right after a windshield chip turns into a crack or a lease notice shows up in the mail. Dealerships are happy to steer that conversation toward the monthly payment, because a lower payment closes a sale faster. That's not the number that matters most. The real answer depends on your tax situation, how you use the vehicle, and what happens to your cash flow over the next three to six years. We've walked dozens of Westport and Fairfield County clients through this decision, from real estate agents who log 20,000 miles a year showing properties to video production owners hauling gear to shoots, and the right answer rarely matches the dealership's recommendation.

This isn't a car blog checklist. It's the framework a CPA actually uses when a client asks, over coffee or on a call, "should I lease or buy?"

## Why the monthly payment is the wrong starting point

A lease payment is almost always lower than a loan payment on the same vehicle, because you're only financing the depreciation during the lease term, not the full purchase price. That makes leasing look cheaper on paper. But the monthly payment doesn't tell you what happens at mileage limits, at lease-end wear charges, or at the moment you want to modify the vehicle for business use. It also ignores the tax treatment, which for a self-employed professional or small business owner can be worth thousands of dollars a year, in either direction.

Start instead with three questions: How is the vehicle actually used? Is it titled personally or through a business? And how long do you typically keep a car before you're itching to trade it in? Those answers, not the sticker payment, determine which option wins.

## The tax picture looks different once a business is involved

If you're an employee driving a personal vehicle, the tax code doesn't offer much of a lever anymore. The 2017 tax law suspended unreimbursed employee business expense deductions through 2025, so commuting and even some work-related mileage as a W-2 employee generally isn't deductible. That changes the moment self-employment, an LLC, or an S-corp enters the picture.

For business owners, real estate agents, and professional service providers who use a vehicle for client meetings, property showings, or job sites, the deduction math depends on whether you buy or lease and how the vehicle is classified.

**If you buy:** you can generally depreciate the vehicle over its useful life, and heavier vehicles may qualify for accelerated write-offs. Under [IRS Publication 946](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p946), vehicles with a gross vehicle weight rating (GVWR) over 6,000 pounds, think many SUVs and pickup trucks, can qualify for a Section 179 deduction of up to $30,500 in 2024, on top of standard depreciation. Lighter passenger vehicles face annual depreciation caps under the luxury auto rules, so the payoff is smaller and slower.

**If you lease:** you generally deduct the business-use percentage of your lease payments each year, plus a small "lease inclusion" adjustment if the vehicle's value exceeds IRS thresholds. There's no big first-year Section 179 windfall, but the deduction is steady and simple to track.

Neither path is automatically better. A real estate agent who trades vehicles every two to three years and wants predictable, level deductions often does better leasing. A contractor who needs a heavy-duty truck for five-plus years and wants the largest possible first-year write-off often does better buying and electing Section 179. This is exactly the kind of decision we work through with clients in our [tax planning](/services/tax) engagements, because the right answer changes based on your entity type, your income level, and what else is happening on your return that year.

## The cash flow test that matters more than the payment

Even after the tax analysis, cash flow decides the winner more often than not. Leasing preserves cash today: lower monthly payments, little or no equity built, and a built-in upgrade cycle every two to four years. Buying ties up cash today (or increases financed debt) in exchange for equity and, eventually, a paid-off asset with no payment at all.

Auto financing costs have moved meaningfully over the past few years. The Federal Reserve's [G.19 Consumer Credit report](https://www.federalreserve.gov/releases/g19/current/) tracks outstanding auto loan balances and terms nationally, and financing costs directly affect which option makes sense for you. When rates are elevated, the gap between a lease payment and a loan payment on the same vehicle widens, which is exactly when leasing starts looking more attractive on cash flow alone, even before you factor in taxes.

Here's a simplified side-by-side to use as a starting point, not a final answer:

| Factor | Leasing | Buying |
|---|---|---|
| Monthly payment | Typically lower | Typically higher |
| Equity built | None | Yes, over time |
| Mileage limits | Usually 10,000–15,000/year | None |
| Tax deduction (business use) | Deduct lease payment percentage | Depreciation, possible Section 179 |
| Best for | Vehicles replaced every 2–3 years | Vehicles kept 5+ years |
| End-of-term flexibility | Turn in, buy out, or re-lease | Sell, trade, or keep driving |

## Driving habits change the answer more than people expect

A client who drives 8,000 miles a year commuting into a home office in Westport has a very different equation than one showing properties across Fairfield County or hauling video equipment between Bay Area shoots. Mileage caps on leases (typically 10,000 to 15,000 miles a year, with per-mile overage charges around 15 to 30 cents) can turn an attractive lease payment into an expensive surprise at turn-in. High-mileage drivers, including many of the real estate professionals we work with, are frequently better off buying, both for the tax depreciation and to avoid overage penalties that can run into the thousands.

On the other hand, a professional service provider who drives mostly for client meetings within a 20-mile radius and likes driving a newer, better-equipped vehicle every couple of years often comes out ahead leasing, especially once the business-use lease deduction is factored in.

We also see this decision get more complicated once entity structure enters the picture, since whether the vehicle sits on a personal return, a Schedule C, or inside an S-corp changes both the deduction mechanics and the audit exposure. ## Common Questions

**Is it ever better to buy a car outright instead of financing or leasing?**
Yes, if you have the cash available and don't need it for other purposes, buying outright eliminates interest cost entirely and gives you full ownership and depreciation rights from day one. For business owners in a high-income year, that can also mean a larger Section 179 deduction than financing would allow, since the deduction is tied to the vehicle's cost basis, not how it's paid for.

**Can I deduct my car lease if I'm self-employed?**
Generally yes, for the business-use percentage of the lease payment, tracked through mileage logs or a consistent business-use percentage. The IRS also requires an income inclusion adjustment on higher-value leased vehicles, which reduces the deduction slightly. [IRS Publication 946](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p946) covers the related depreciation rules for owned vehicles, and a CPA can walk through the lease-specific adjustment for your situation.

**Does the Section 179 vehicle deduction apply to any car I buy for my business?**
No. Section 179 favors heavier vehicles, generally those with a GVWR over 6,000 pounds, which is why many SUVs and trucks qualify for larger first-year deductions than sedans. Lighter passenger vehicles fall under separate luxury auto depreciation limits that cap the deduction significantly.

**How do interest rates affect the lease vs. buy decision right now?**
Higher auto financing rates widen the gap between loan and lease payments, since lease pricing is driven more by projected depreciation than by the prevailing interest rate. The Federal Reserve's [G.19 report](https://www.federalreserve.gov/releases/g19/current/) tracks these financing trends, and when rates climb, the cash flow argument for leasing tends to get stronger, even before tax considerations come into play.
