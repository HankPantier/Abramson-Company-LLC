A client called us last April convinced she deserved a deduction for the 200 hours she spent coordinating a food drive at her church in Westport. Another client swore the opposite: that none of what he'd spent volunteering at an equine rescue in Wilton could possibly count. Both were partly right and partly wrong, and that mix-up is one of the most common tax questions we hear between January and April.

Here's the short version: the IRS will never let you deduct the value of your time or services, no matter how skilled you are or how many hours you gave. But the money you spend out of pocket while doing that volunteer work, for a qualified organization, is often deductible if you itemize and keep the right paperwork. The confusion isn't really about the law. It's about which costs count, which don't, and what records the IRS expects to see if it asks.

## Your hours have value, but the IRS doesn't count them

A CPA who volunteers ten hours preparing a nonprofit's financial statements can't deduct a professional hourly rate for that time, even though the same work would cost the organization real money if it hired someone. A contractor who donates a weekend rebuilding a Habitat for Humanity porch can't deduct the fair value of his labor either. [IRS Publication 526](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p526) is direct about this: the value of your time or services is never deductible, full stop.

What changes the math is spending your own money to do the volunteering. Materials, mileage, parking, supplies, even a required uniform, those are out-of-pocket costs, and the IRS treats them differently than your time. The organization has to be a qualified 501(c)(3), and you can look one up through the [IRS Charities & Nonprofits Resource Hub](https://www.irs.gov/charities-non-profits) if you're not sure your favorite cause qualifies.

## The out-of-pocket costs that qualify for a deduction

Unreimbursed volunteer expenses fall into a handful of categories, and most people only know about one or two.

Supplies you buy and never get reimbursed for count. A parent who buys $180 in craft supplies for a nonprofit after-school art program in Norwalk can deduct that $180. A board member who prints and mails fundraising letters at her own expense can deduct the paper, postage, and printing.

Uniforms count too, but only if they're not suitable for everyday wear. A Red Cross volunteer's official uniform qualifies. A plain white polo shirt you bought to look presentable at a gala does not, because you could wear it anywhere.

Travel away from home for volunteer work can qualify if the trip has no significant element of personal vacation. A retail business owner who flies to Houston for a week to help a disaster relief nonprofit gut and rebuild flooded homes can generally deduct airfare, lodging, and meals for that trip, so long as the days are genuinely spent working and not sightseeing.

What doesn't count: babysitting costs so you can volunteer, the value of skilled services (legal advice, bookkeeping, medical care) you donate for free, and any expense reimbursed by the organization. If the nonprofit pays you back for gas, you can't also deduct the mileage.

## Mileage: the deduction most volunteers miss

This is the one that surprises almost everyone, including longtime donors. The charitable mileage rate has been frozen by statute at 14 cents per mile for years, far below the standard business mileage rate the IRS adjusts annually (67 cents per mile for 2024). Congress sets the charitable rate, not the IRS, which is why it hasn't moved in decades even as gas prices have.

| Purpose | 2024 rate per mile |
|---|---|
| Charitable/volunteer driving | 14 cents |
| Business driving | 67 cents |
| Medical or moving (active military) | 21 cents |

Fourteen cents doesn't sound like much, but it adds up. A volunteer driving 40 miles round trip twice a week to deliver Meals on Wheels in Fairfield County racks up over 4,000 miles a year, which is roughly a $580 deduction. Parking fees and tolls paid during that driving are separately deductible in full, on top of the mileage.

You have a choice each year between deducting actual gas and oil costs directly tied to the volunteer driving, or using the 14-cent flat rate. Most people come out ahead with the flat rate, but if you're doing significant mileage in an older, less fuel-efficient vehicle, it's worth running both numbers.

## Keep records like the IRS might actually ask

The deduction rules aren't complicated, but the recordkeeping is where most volunteers fall short, and it's the first thing that gets challenged in an audit.

For any single out-of-pocket expense of $250 or more, Publication 526 requires a written acknowledgment from the organization describing the expense and confirming you received no goods or services in return. A canceled check or bank statement alone won't satisfy that $250 threshold. Under $250, a receipt or your own written record of the date, amount, and purpose is generally enough, but a simple log is still worth keeping.

For mileage, track the date, destination, purpose, and miles driven for every trip, not just an estimated annual total reconstructed in March. A mileage log app or a notebook kept in the glove compartment works fine. What doesn't work is guessing at tax time how many Tuesdays you drove to the shelter last year.

We tell clients to treat volunteer expense records the same way they'd treat receipts for a business deduction: keep them for at least three years after filing, and don't wait until you're sitting across from us in February to start organizing them.

## Board members and nonprofit volunteers carry extra risk

A lot of our clients in Westport and Fairfield County sit on nonprofit boards, whether it's a local arts organization, a youth sports league, or an animal rescue. Board service brings its own wrinkle: expenses you incur specifically because of your board role, like travel to a board retreat or printing costs for a fundraising campaign you personally ran, follow the same unreimbursed-expense rules above. But board members also need to understand the organization's own filing obligations, because a board member's personal deduction depends on the nonprofit actually being in good standing as a qualified 501(c)(3).

## Common Questions

**Can I deduct the value of my time spent volunteering?**
No. The IRS explicitly disallows a deduction for the value of your time or services, no matter your profession or the market rate for that work. Only unreimbursed, out-of-pocket costs connected to the volunteer work can be deducted, and only if you itemize.

**What's the mileage rate for driving to volunteer?**
The charitable mileage rate is 14 cents per mile, a rate set by Congress rather than adjusted annually like the business mileage rate. Parking fees and tolls incurred during that driving are separately deductible in full.

**Do I need a receipt for every volunteer expense?**
For any single expense of $250 or more, you need a written acknowledgment from the organization confirming the expense and that you received nothing in return. Below $250, a receipt or a written log with the date, amount, and purpose is generally sufficient.

**Can I deduct expenses for volunteering with any charity I choose?**
Only if the organization is a qualified 501(c)(3) under IRS rules. You can confirm an organization's status through the [IRS Charities & Nonprofits Resource Hub](https://www.irs.gov/charities-non-profits) before assuming your expenses will qualify.
