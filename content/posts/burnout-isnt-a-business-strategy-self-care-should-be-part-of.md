Ray started Abramson & Company in 2005 after watching too many good business owners run themselves into the ground trying to prove they could do it all. Twenty years later, the conversation hasn't changed much. A retail owner in downtown Westport works six days a week through the holiday season and calls it dedication. A real estate agent in Fairfield County answers calls at 9 p.m. because a deal might fall apart if she doesn't. An equine business owner does the books at midnight after a full day in the barn, because that's the only quiet hour she has. Every one of them would tell you exhaustion is just the cost of doing business.

It isn't. It's a cost to the business, and it shows up on the balance sheet whether an owner tracks it or not. Missed deadlines, late fees, a payroll mistake that triggers a state notice, a contract signed without reading the fine print because it's 11 p.m. and the owner just wants to be done. None of that is a strategy problem. It's a capacity problem, and capacity is something a CPA firm can actually help protect.

## The real cost of running on empty

Owners tend to measure burnout in feelings: tired, irritable, checked out. Lenders and accountants measure it differently. When an owner is depleted, decision quality drops, and decision quality is what keeps a business solvent. A distracted owner misses a quarterly estimated tax payment and eats a penalty. An overextended owner skips reviewing a vendor contract and locks in a bad rate for eighteen months. A sleep-deprived owner approves a hire without checking cash flow projections, and three months later payroll is the thing keeping them up at night.

None of these are hypothetical. They're the kind of situations that show up in client meetings at Abramson & Company every tax season, across industries from professional services to video production. The pattern is consistent: the mistakes owners make when they're running on fumes are rarely small, and they're almost always avoidable with a second set of eyes. That's a big part of why bookkeeping errors and cash shortfalls compound the way they do, a topic covered in more depth in [why small businesses are going bankrupt and how to avoid it](/resources/why-small-businesses-are-going-bankrupt-and-how-to-avoid-it).

## Decision fatigue is a financial liability, not a mood

Here's the part most owners miss: judgment is a finite resource, and every business decision draws from the same pool. Pricing a new service, negotiating a lease renewal, deciding whether to take on a new client who pays late but pays well, choosing whether to expand into a second location. Each of those decisions deserves a clear head. An owner who hasn't taken a real day off in four months isn't bringing a clear head to any of them.

This is where a lot of owners equate rest with weakness. They see delegation as an admission that they can't handle it, when in practice, delegation is what lets the important decisions get the attention they need. An owner who hands bookkeeping, payroll, and tax planning to a CPA-led team isn't stepping back from the business. They're freeing up the mental bandwidth to make the calls only they can make: which clients to keep, which markets to enter, when to hire.

## Building recovery into the growth plan

A sustainable growth plan treats rest the same way it treats cash reserves: as something you build in on purpose, not something you hope you'll have left over. That means blocking real time off the calendar before the year starts, not after a health scare forces the issue. It means setting a hard stop on evening emails, at least most nights. It means having systems and people in place so the business doesn't require the owner's constant presence to function.

The SBA's guidance on [managing your business](https://www.sba.gov/business-guide/manage-your-business) makes a similar point: sustainable operations depend on systems, not on any one person working harder than everyone else. A business that only runs because the owner never stops is not a stable business. It's a single point of failure with a payroll account attached.

Small, concrete habits matter more than sweeping resolutions. An owner who reviews financials once a week for twenty minutes, instead of once a quarter in a panic, catches problems while they're still cheap to fix. An owner who tracks the same three or four numbers consistently, rather than drowning in a spreadsheet with forty tabs, actually uses the data. That kind of discipline is covered in [the new KPIs of business](/resources/the-new-KPIs-of-business), and it applies as much to protecting an owner's time as it does to protecting margin.

## Common Questions

**Is self-care really a financial issue, or is that a stretch?**
It's a financial issue in a direct, measurable way. Decision fatigue leads to missed deadlines, penalty payments, bad hires, and contracts signed without proper review. A CPA who sees the downstream cost of these mistakes across dozens of clients a year has a clear view of how burnout turns into real dollars.

**How much time off is realistic for a small business owner?**
There's no universal number, but the principle matters more than the count: time off needs to be scheduled in advance and protected the same way a tax deadline is protected. Owners who wait for a slow month to finally rest usually find that month never comes.

**What's the fastest way to reduce financial stress without hiring a full accounting department?**
Bundling bookkeeping, payroll, and tax planning with one CPA-led team, rather than piecing together separate vendors, cuts down on both cost and mental overhead. A fixed monthly fee also means an owner can ask a quick question without worrying about a surprise bill.

**Does this apply to solo entrepreneurs, or just larger companies with staff?**
It applies especially to solo entrepreneurs and owner-operators, since there's no one else to catch a missed deadline or a bookkeeping error. Influencers, video production freelancers, and single-agent real estate businesses are some of the clients who benefit most from having a second set of eyes on the numbers.
