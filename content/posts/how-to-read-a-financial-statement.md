## The three core financial statements explained

Three reports do almost all the work of showing you how your business is really doing, and each one answers a different question.

The **balance sheet** answers "what do I own and owe right now?" It lists assets (cash, equipment, receivables), liabilities (loans, credit lines, unpaid bills), and equity, the difference between the two. Think of it as a snapshot taken on one specific date.

The **income statement**, sometimes called a profit and loss statement, answers "did I make money over this period?" It shows revenue coming in, expenses going out, and the profit or loss left over. Unlike the balance sheet, it covers a stretch of time, a month, a quarter, or a year.

The **cash flow statement** answers the question owners get wrong most often: "where did my actual cash go?" A business can show a profit on paper and still run short on cash because of loan payments, inventory purchases, or slow-paying clients. This report tracks money moving in and out of operations, investing, and financing activities, and it's often the most revealing of the three.

## What to look for as a real estate, retail, equine, or service business owner

The line items that matter most shift depending on what you sell and how you get paid. Here's where to focus first.

### Real Estate
icon: Home

Watch cash flow timing against closing schedules, commission splits, and carrying costs on listings. A profitable quarter on paper can still leave you cash-poor while deals are pending.

### Retail Businesses
icon: Building2

Inventory turnover and gross margin tell you more than total revenue. A retailer with rising sales but shrinking margin is usually sitting on markdowns or slow-moving stock.

### Equine Businesses
icon: HeartPulse

Boarding, training, and breeding income often blend on one income statement. Separating them, alongside feed, vet, and facility costs, shows which part of the operation is actually profitable.

### Professional Services and Influencers
icon: Briefcase

For consultants, attorneys, and influencers, accounts receivable and billable utilization matter more than revenue alone. A healthy income statement with unpaid invoices piling up on the balance sheet is a warning sign, not a win.

## Common mistakes business owners make reading their reports

We see the same handful of errors across real estate offices, retail shops, and service firms in Fairfield County. Most are avoidable once someone points them out.

- Confusing profit with cash. A strong income statement doesn't guarantee money in the bank; the cash flow statement tells that story instead.
- Ignoring the balance sheet entirely. Owners who only track revenue miss rising debt or shrinking equity until it's a crisis.
- Reading statements once a year. Monthly or quarterly review catches problems while they're still cheap to fix.
- Overlooking accounts receivable aging. Money owed to you isn't money you have; unpaid invoices can mask a real cash shortage.
- Comparing the wrong periods. A seasonal retail or equine business needs year-over-year comparisons, not just month-to-month, to mean anything.

## Frequently Asked Questions About How to read a financial statement

**Q: What are the three main financial statements?**
A: The three core statements are the balance sheet, income statement, and cash flow statement. The balance sheet shows what a business owns and owes on a specific date, the income statement shows profit or loss over a period, and the cash flow statement tracks actual cash movement.

**Q: Why does my income statement show a profit but I have no cash?**
A: Profit and cash are different measures. Your income statement counts revenue when it's earned, even if a client hasn't paid yet, while loan payments, inventory purchases, and unpaid invoices affect your cash balance separately. The cash flow statement explains the gap.

**Q: How often should a small business review its financial statements?**
A: Monthly or quarterly review catches problems while they're inexpensive to fix. Waiting until year-end, which many Westport small business owners do, often means a cash shortage or margin problem has already grown into something harder to reverse.
