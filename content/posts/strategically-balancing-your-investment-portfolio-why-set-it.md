A client came to us three years ago with a portfolio that looked great on paper: steady returns, a name-brand fund lineup, nothing dramatic. When we pulled it apart, it turned out the equity allocation had drifted from 65% to nearly 82% because he hadn't touched it since 2019. He wasn't wrong to like the returns. But he was carrying far more risk than he realized, and one bad quarter would have hit him much harder than he expected. That's the quiet cost of "set it and forget it" investing: it feels responsible because you're not making impulsive trades, but the portfolio is drifting anyway, just without anyone watching.

A balanced investment portfolio isn't a single decision you make once and revisit at retirement. It's a discipline, closer to maintaining a house than framing a photograph. Markets move, some holdings grow faster than others, and your own life changes shape what "balanced" even means for you. Skipping the maintenance doesn't preserve your original strategy. It slowly replaces it with something else.

## Why allocations drift even when you do nothing

Asset allocation strategy starts with a mix, say 60% stocks and 40% bonds, chosen to match your goals and your tolerance for risk. That mix is only accurate on day one. If stocks return 10% annually and bonds return 3%, a 60/40 portfolio left untouched can look like this within a few years:

| Year | Stock allocation (untouched) | Bond allocation (untouched) |
|------|------------------------------|------------------------------|
| 0 | 60% | 40% |
| 3 | 68% | 32% |
| 5 | 74% | 26% |
| 7 | 79% | 21% |

The portfolio has grown, which feels good. But the risk profile has changed just as much. By year seven, someone who chose 60/40 because they wanted moderate risk is now running a portfolio that behaves like an aggressive growth account, without ever deciding to take on that risk. The Federal Reserve's research notes on household finance and investment behavior have tracked how allocation shifts like this tend to go unnoticed by individual investors for years at a time, particularly during extended bull markets when nobody wants to question a winning streak. You can review that research directly through the [Federal Reserve's notes on household finance and investment behavior](https://www.federalreserve.gov/econres/notes/feds-notes/default.htm).

## What rebalancing actually looks like in practice

Portfolio rebalancing means selling a portion of what's grown and adding to what hasn't, to bring the mix back to your target. It sounds counterintuitive, since you're trimming your winners. That's the point. You're not predicting which asset class will do better next year. You're making sure no single decision from three years ago is quietly running your entire financial plan today.

Most of our Westport and Fairfield County clients rebalance on one of two schedules: a calendar check (once or twice a year) or a threshold check (rebalance whenever an asset class drifts more than 5 percentage points from target). Both work. What doesn't work is doing it only when the market drops and fear finally forces a decision. By then you're rebalancing under stress, often at the worst possible moment.

The part investors miss most often is the tax side. Selling appreciated stock inside a taxable brokerage account triggers capital gains, and a rebalancing move made without a tax plan can cost more than the risk it was meant to fix. A CPA-led review can time trades around loss harvesting, use new contributions to rebalance instead of selling, and coordinate rebalancing with year-end tax planning rather than treating it as a separate event. If you haven't looked at how taxes and investment decisions interact this year, our guide on [4 steps to get ahead of higher taxes](/resources/4-steps-to-get-ahead-of-higher-taxes) is a useful starting point.

## Risk tolerance investing changes with your life, not just the market

Risk tolerance isn't a fixed number you calculate once at age 30 and carry forever. It shifts with your income, your family, and your business. A Westport small business owner in growth mode can usually absorb more portfolio volatility, since the business itself is the primary asset generating cash flow. That same owner five years from a sale, with retirement funds tied up in a 401(k) and a chunk of net worth locked in the company, typically needs a more conservative personal portfolio to offset the concentration risk already sitting in the business.

Real estate professionals face a similar mismatch. Property income and personal investment accounts often move in correlated directions during a downturn, which means a portfolio that looks diversified on a statement can behave less diversified in an actual recession. Retail business owners carrying seasonal cash flow swings need liquidity considerations that a static 70/30 model doesn't account for. None of this shows up automatically in a rebalancing algorithm. It shows up in a conversation about what's actually happening in your business and your household.

The AICPA's Personal Financial Planning resources point to the same conclusion from a different angle: financial planning works best as an ongoing relationship between tax, investment, and life-event decisions, not a set of separate documents reviewed once a year. You can see their guidance directly at the [AICPA Personal Financial Planning resource hub](https://www.aicpa-cima.com/topic/personal-financial-planning).

## Portfolio diversification means more than owning different funds

A lot of portfolios that look diversified on the surface aren't. Five mutual funds that all hold the same 20 large-cap technology names don't reduce risk the way five genuinely different asset classes do. True portfolio diversification spreads exposure across asset types (equities, fixed income, real assets, cash), across geographies, and across correlation, meaning holdings that don't all rise and fall together.

This matters more for business owners than most people assume. If your income, your retirement account, and your business equity are all tied to the same local economy or the same sector, a downturn hits every part of your financial picture at once. Diversifying the investment portfolio is one of the few levers available to offset that concentration, and it only works if someone is checking the actual overlap between holdings, not just counting how many fund names appear on a statement.

## Common Questions

**How often should I rebalance my investment portfolio?**
Most investors do well rebalancing on a set schedule, once or twice a year, or whenever an asset class drifts more than about 5 percentage points from its target. Rebalancing more frequently than that usually adds transaction costs and tax events without meaningfully improving results.

**Does rebalancing always mean selling investments and triggering taxes?**
Not necessarily. New contributions, dividends, and interest can often be directed toward underweighted asset classes instead of selling appreciated positions, which avoids triggering capital gains. When selling is unavoidable, timing it alongside loss harvesting or year-end tax planning usually reduces the tax hit.

**How is asset allocation different from diversification?**
Asset allocation is the mix between broad categories like stocks, bonds, and cash. Diversification is how spread out your holdings are within and across those categories. A portfolio can have a reasonable stock-bond split and still be poorly diversified if most of the stock holdings overlap in the same sector or region.

**Does my risk tolerance change if I own a business?**
Often, yes. Business owners frequently carry concentrated risk in the business itself, which changes how much additional risk the rest of the portfolio should hold. Someone with significant equity tied up in a company usually benefits from a more conservative personal investment mix to balance that concentration.
