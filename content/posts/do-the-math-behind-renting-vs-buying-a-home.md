Ask five people in Westport whether they should buy or rent, and you'll get five different answers rooted in feelings: fear of missing the market, dread of "throwing money away" on rent, or the itch to finally own something with their name on it. None of that is wrong exactly, but none of it is math either. Whether you buy or rent a home in Fairfield County, or anywhere else, comes down to a financial statement question with a specific answer for your specific numbers. Treat it that way and the emotional noise gets a lot quieter.

## Build a real balance sheet before you build an opinion

Most people compare a mortgage payment to a rent check and stop there. That's an income statement view, and it leaves out half the picture. A proper comparison puts your down payment, closing costs, ongoing maintenance, insurance, and property taxes on one side, and your rent, renter's insurance, and the return you'd earn by investing the cash you didn't spend on a down payment on the other. If you've read our guide on [how to read a financial statement](/resources/how-to-read-a-financial-statement), you already know the habit: look at assets and liabilities together, not just the monthly cash outflow. The same discipline applies here.

## What renting actually costs vs what owning actually costs

Here's a realistic example using Westport-area numbers. Say you're deciding between a $4,200-a-month rental and an $850,000 home with 20% down ($170,000).

| Cost category | Renting | Owning |
|---|---|---|
| Monthly housing payment | $4,200 | ~$4,300 (principal + interest at 6.5%, 30-year) |
| Property tax | $0 | ~$1,000/month |
| Homeowners/renters insurance | ~$30/month | ~$170/month |
| Maintenance and repairs | $0 | ~$700/month (roughly 1% of home value annually) |
| HOA or condo fees | Varies | Varies |
| **Estimated monthly total** | **~$4,230** | **~$6,170** |

The owning column doesn't include the $170,000 down payment or roughly $17,000 to $42,500 in closing costs (2% to 5% of the purchase price). It also doesn't include the equity you build with every principal payment, which is the whole reason people buy in the first place. That equity has real value. It's just not free, and it's not instant.

## The mortgage interest deduction is smaller than most owners assume

A lot of buyers count on the mortgage interest deduction to tilt the math in their favor. For many households, it barely moves the needle. Under current law, mortgage interest is deductible on up to $750,000 of acquisition debt, and only if you itemize instead of taking the standard deduction. See [IRS Topic No. 505](https://www.irs.gov/taxtopics/tc505) for the specifics. With the standard deduction sitting at $29,200 for married couples filing jointly in 2024, a lot of homeowners with smaller mortgages or lower interest rates find their itemized deductions don't clear that bar by much, if at all. That doesn't make owning a bad move. It does mean the tax benefit shouldn't be the deciding factor in your spreadsheet.

## Your down payment has an opportunity cost, not just a price tag

That $170,000 down payment isn't sitting in a drawer if you rent instead. Invested at a conservative 6% average annual return, it grows to roughly $228,000 over five years, and that's before accounting for the monthly savings if renting costs less than owning in your market. This is the calculation people skip most often, and it's the one that changes the answer for a lot of Westport families. Money used as a down payment stops compounding somewhere else. That's not a reason to avoid buying. It's a cost that belongs in the comparison.

## How long you plan to stay changes which side wins

Buying involves upfront costs that renting doesn't: closing costs, inspection fees, moving expenses. Selling involves more: real estate commissions typically run 5% to 6% of the sale price, plus any repairs a buyer negotiates. If you sell that $850,000 home three years after buying it, transaction costs alone could run $50,000 to $90,000, money that has to be recovered through appreciation and equity before ownership pulls ahead. The general rule of thumb is that owning starts to beat renting somewhere between years five and seven, depending on your local market and mortgage rate. If your job, family situation, or lifestyle points to a move within three years, renting usually wins on the math alone, no matter how the emotional argument feels.

## National net worth data is worth knowing, but it isn't your answer

The [Federal Reserve's Survey of Consumer Finances](https://www.federalreserve.gov/publications/files/scf20.pdf) shows homeowners consistently report far higher median net worth than renters. It's tempting to read that as "buy a house and get rich." The data doesn't say that. Homeowners tend to have higher incomes, longer time horizons, and more stable finances to begin with, which is part of why they qualify to buy in the first place. The statistic describes a population, not a guarantee. Your household's numbers, not a national survey, should decide your move.

## Make this part of a bigger financial plan, not a one-time decision

Buying a home isn't an isolated event. It affects your cash flow, your tax return, your retirement savings rate, and your ability to handle an unexpected expense. ## Common Questions

**Is renting actually better than buying financially?**
It depends on your timeline, your local rent-to-price ratio, and what you'd do with the money you'd otherwise put into a down payment. If you plan to stay fewer than five years, or renting costs meaningfully less than owning a comparable home each month, renting often wins financially even though buying builds equity over time.

**How do I calculate whether buying makes sense for me?**
Compare your all-in monthly ownership cost (mortgage principal and interest, property tax, insurance, and roughly 1% of home value annually for maintenance) against your rent plus the investment return you'd earn on the down payment and closing costs you'd otherwise spend. Run that comparison over your expected time in the home, not just year one.

**Does the mortgage interest deduction make buying worth it?**
For many households, not by much. You need to itemize deductions to claim it, mortgage interest is limited to the first $750,000 of acquisition debt, and the 2024 standard deduction of $29,200 for joint filers means a lot of homeowners don't clear that threshold significantly. Check [IRS Topic No. 505](https://www.irs.gov/taxtopics/tc505) for the current rules before you count on it.

**How long do I need to stay in a home to make buying worth it?**
A common range is five to seven years, since closing costs on the purchase (2% to 5%) and selling costs later (5% to 6% in commissions) need time and appreciation to offset. Shorter timelines usually favor renting.
