Nobody plans for long-term care the way they plan for a mortgage or a child's college fund, and that's exactly the problem. By the time a parent needs help bathing, dressing, or managing medication, the family is usually making decisions under pressure, often paying retail rates for care that could have been funded years earlier at a fraction of the stress. Long-term care is not a medical emergency you insure against after the fact. It's a financial risk you manage in advance, the same way you'd manage liability exposure or a concentrated stock position. For families in Westport and across Fairfield County, where home health aide rates run higher than the national average, the earlier this conversation happens, the more options stay on the table.

This post walks through what long-term care actually costs, how insurance stacks up against self-funding, where the VA Aid and Attendance benefit fits for veterans and surviving spouses, and how to build a funding plan that blends all three instead of betting everything on one.

## What long-term care actually costs

Most people underestimate this by a wide margin. National data consistently shows a private room in a nursing home running well over $100,000 a year, with assisted living facilities averaging somewhere in the $60,000 to $70,000 range annually, and a full-time home health aide often costing $75,000 or more a year depending on hours and skill level. In Fairfield County, home care and facility rates typically run above these national figures because of local wage costs and demand.

Here's the number that catches most families off guard: average care duration. Someone who needs long-term care doesn't need it for six months. Multi-year stays are common, and dementia-related care frequently extends five years or longer. Multiply even a conservative $85,000 annual cost by three or four years, and you're looking at a $250,000 to $350,000 expense that most retirement plans never explicitly budgeted for.

## Long-term care insurance vs self-funding

There are really three ways to pay for long-term care: a dedicated insurance policy, savings and investments earmarked for the purpose, or a hybrid that uses both. Each has real tradeoffs, and the right answer depends on age, health, family history, and the size of the estate being protected.

| Factor | Long-term care insurance | Self-funding from savings |
|---|---|---|
| Best time to start | Age 50-65, while still insurable | Any age, but needs decades to build |
| Predictability | Fixed premium, defined benefit | Depends on market performance and timing |
| Underwriting risk | Denied or priced high if health declines | No underwriting required |
| Estate impact | Preserves other assets for heirs | Directly depletes savings or investments |
| Flexibility | Benefits tied to policy terms | Full control over how funds are spent |
| Inflation protection | Available as a rider, at added cost | Depends on investment growth outpacing costs |

Insurance makes the most sense for people in their mid-50s to mid-60s who are still healthy enough to qualify and who want to protect a specific pool of assets, such as a business or a home they intend to pass down. Premiums have risen sharply over the past decade, so getting quotes earlier rather than later matters more than it used to.

Self-funding works best for households with substantial investment portfolios who can absorb a six-figure care expense without derailing retirement income. It requires discipline: the money has to actually be earmarked, not folded into a general investment account and hoped for later. If your portfolio strategy hasn't been stress-tested against a scenario like this, it's worth revisiting how your [balanced investment portfolio](/resources/strategically-balancing-your-investment-portfolio-why-set-it) is positioned for a large, unplanned withdrawal a decade or two out.

Most of our clients land on a hybrid: a smaller insurance policy or hybrid life/LTC product to cover a base level of risk, paired with dedicated savings to cover the gap. This spreads the risk instead of concentrating it in one strategy that might not hold up.

## The VA benefit veterans and surviving spouses often miss

For veterans and their surviving spouses, there's a funding source that gets overlooked constantly: the [VA Aid and Attendance and Housebound benefit](https://www.va.gov/pension/aid-attendance-housebound/). This is an increased monthly pension amount available to eligible wartime veterans, and their surviving spouses, who need help with daily activities like bathing, dressing, or managing medications, or who are housebound.

The eligibility rules involve wartime service dates, income limits net of unreimbursed medical expenses, and net worth thresholds, so this isn't a benefit you self-diagnose from a web search. But for a veteran family already facing $70,000-plus in annual home care costs, an additional benefit in the range of several hundred to over $2,000 a month, depending on marital status and disability level, changes the funding math meaningfully. If there's a veteran or a surviving spouse anywhere in the family tree who might need care in the next five to ten years, it's worth checking eligibility now, before the need is urgent and the application is being rushed.

## Building a funding plan that blends all three

A long-term care funding strategy that actually holds up usually layers three pieces together instead of leaning on one:

1. **A baseline insurance or hybrid policy** purchased while still insurable, sized to cover a meaningful chunk of the projected annual cost rather than all of it.
2. **A dedicated savings or investment bucket**, separate from retirement income accounts, built specifically to cover the gap between insurance benefits and actual costs.
3. **Benefit eligibility review**, including VA Aid and Attendance for veteran households, checked and documented well before care is needed.

The order matters. Insurance underwriting gets harder and more expensive with age and health changes, so that decision should happen first, ideally in your 50s or early 60s. Savings targets can then be calculated as the gap between what insurance will pay and what care will actually cost in your area. Benefit eligibility should be confirmed on paper, not assumed, because income and net worth limits shift and documentation requirements are specific.

This is also where the estate and tax picture connects. Some long-term care expenses and a portion of qualified long-term care insurance premiums are deductible as medical expenses if they exceed the applicable threshold of adjusted gross income, as outlined in [IRS Publication 502](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p502). That deduction won't fund the whole plan, but for households already itemizing, it's real money that shouldn't be left on the table. Coordinating this with your broader tax strategy, the same way you would with any major deduction, is part of why long-term care planning belongs in the same conversation as your annual [tax planning](/resources/maximizing-your-tax-refund-a-strategic-guide-to-tax-planning) rather than treated as a separate, standalone decision.

## How to start without feeling overwhelmed

Start with three numbers: your current age, your family health history, and a rough estimate of investable assets outside your primary residence. Those three data points determine whether insurance, self-funding, or a blend makes sense, and whether VA benefits apply to anyone in the household. From there, a real plan involves an insurance quote comparison, a savings target tied to actual local care costs, and a benefits eligibility check, not a single decision made in a single afternoon.

This is also a good moment to look at your overall financial picture rather than long-term care in isolation. If retirement income, insurance coverage, and investment allocation haven't been reviewed together recently, a broader [financial planning](/resources/5-tips-to-build-a-healthy-money-mindset-and-grow-your-finances) conversation often surfaces gaps that a long-term care discussion alone would miss.

## Common Questions

**How much does long-term care typically cost per year?**
National estimates put a private nursing home room well above $100,000 a year, assisted living in the $60,000-$70,000 range, and a full-time home health aide around $75,000 or more annually. Costs in Fairfield County typically run higher than these national figures due to local wage and demand levels.

**Is long-term care insurance worth it if I'm already in my 60s?**
It depends on health status and how much of your estate you're trying to protect. Premiums rise with age and underwriting gets stricter, so it's worth getting quotes now rather than waiting, but a hybrid life/LTC product or a self-funding strategy may make more sense if health issues have already surfaced.

**Can veterans get help paying for long-term care?**
Eligible wartime veterans and surviving spouses may qualify for the VA Aid and Attendance and Housebound benefit, an increased pension amount for those needing help with daily activities. Eligibility depends on service dates, income net of medical expenses, and net worth, detailed at [va.gov](https://www.va.gov/pension/aid-attendance-housebound/).

**Are long-term care expenses tax deductible?**
Some long-term care costs and qualified insurance premiums count as deductible medical expenses once they exceed the applicable percentage of adjusted gross income, per [IRS Publication 502](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p502). Whether this helps depends on your total itemized deductions for the year.

Long-term care is one of the biggest unbudgeted expenses a family will face, and the funding decisions work best when they're made years before they're needed.
