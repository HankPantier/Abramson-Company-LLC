## Why 1099 compliance trips up local business owners

Every January, the same question lands in our inbox: "Do I need to send this vendor a 1099?" It's not a simple yes or no. Between IRS guidance, Connecticut Department of Revenue Services rules, and whatever a business owner's neighbor said at a Westport Farmers' Market stall last weekend, the information is everywhere and none of it fits your specific situation.

Small business owners in Fairfield County run lean. A retail shop in Westport might use a bookkeeper part-time. A real estate professional in Fairfield might handle their own 1099s between showings. Nobody has three hours to read IRS instructions cover to cover, and generic blog posts rarely address the exact mix of contractors, vendors, and payments a given business has on its books.

That's the gap this guide is built to close: plain answers to the questions that actually come up, plus a clear next step when your situation needs a second set of eyes.

## Who needs to receive a 1099 from your business

The general rule: if you paid an unincorporated contractor, freelancer, or business $600 or more in a calendar year for services, you likely owe them a 1099. That threshold applies per payee, per year, and it covers cash, check, or direct deposit payments. Payments made through a third-party network like PayPal or a credit card typically don't count. Those are reported by the payment processor instead.

What trips people up is the contractor-versus-employee line. If you control how, when, and where someone does the work, they're probably an employee who belongs on payroll, not a 1099. Misclassifying a worker creates real exposure with both the IRS and Connecticut DRS.

A few common scenarios we see across our client base:

- A professional service provider hires a freelance bookkeeper or marketing consultant for a project. That's 1099 territory.
- A retail business pays a local contractor to build store fixtures or handle repairs. Also 1099 territory.
- A real estate professional pays a property manager, appraiser, or independent stager. Same rule applies.
- Payments to a corporation (most LLCs taxed as S-corps or C-corps) are generally exempt, with attorneys being a notable exception.

## Which 1099 form applies to your situation

The IRS split what used to be one form into several, and the names alone cause confusion.

**Form 1099-NEC** covers nonemployee compensation. This is the one most small businesses use most often: payments for services from freelancers, consultants, and independent contractors.

**Form 1099-MISC** now covers everything else: rent payments (relevant for landlords and real estate professionals), royalties over $10, prizes and awards, and legal settlements. If you're a landlord paying a property manager a flat management fee for services, that's likely NEC. If you're paying rent to a building owner as a business tenant, that's MISC.

**Form 1099-K** comes from third-party payment processors, not from you directly, and covers payment card and network transactions above IRS thresholds. It's worth understanding even though you won't be the one filing it.

Getting the wrong form to a contractor doesn't just create paperwork. It can trigger IRS mismatch notices months later, long after you've moved on to the next tax season.

## Key deadlines and filing steps for Connecticut businesses

January 31 is the date to circle. Federal 1099-NEC forms are due to both the contractor and the IRS by January 31, whether you file on paper or electronically. Form 1099-MISC follows a split deadline: contractors get their copy by January 31, but the IRS copy isn't due until February 28 on paper or March 31 if filed electronically.

Connecticut adds its own layer. The state requires businesses to file certain 1099s with the Department of Revenue Services, generally aligned with the federal January 31 deadline, and businesses meeting the IRS electronic filing threshold must e-file with the state as well. Missing this step is easy to overlook if you're only thinking about the IRS.

Penalties escalate quickly. Filing late but within 30 days can cost $60 per form; wait past August 1 or skip filing altogether, and per-form penalties climb toward $330, with no cap for intentional disregard. For a business issuing a dozen or more 1099s, that adds up fast, and it's the kind of year-end stress that's entirely avoidable with a system in place before December hits.

## Special considerations for real estate, retail, and influencer clients

Each niche we serve carries its own 1099 wrinkles.

**Real estate:** Landlords who pay property managers, maintenance contractors, or independent appraisers $600 or more generally owe a 1099-NEC. Rent collected by a property manager on your behalf and then passed to you doesn't always require a 1099 between you and the manager, but the manager typically needs to issue 1099s to the contractors they hire on your property. It's a chain that's easy to lose track of across multiple properties.

**Retail:** Stores that lean on a rotating bench of contractors, delivery drivers, visual merchandisers, seasonal help outside of payroll, often underestimate how many 1099s they owe by December. A quick vendor list review in October saves a scramble in January.

**Influencers and content creators:** Brand deal payments, affiliate income, and gifted product with cash value can all generate 1099 obligations, either as the recipient or, if you're running your own small production team, as the issuer. The lines between personal and business income get blurry fast in this space.

## Frequently Asked Questions About Understanding 1099 requirements

**Q: Do I need to send a 1099 to an LLC?**
A: It depends on how the LLC is taxed. If it's taxed as a sole proprietorship or partnership, you generally still owe a 1099 for payments of $600 or more. If it's taxed as an S-corp or C-corp, you usually don't, with attorneys as a notable exception.

**Q: What happens if I file a 1099 late in Connecticut?**
A: Federal penalties start at $60 per form for filings up to 30 days late and can climb to $330 per form for filings after August 1 or not filed at all. Connecticut DRS may apply additional state-level penalties for missed filings.

**Q: Is there a difference between 1099-NEC and 1099-MISC?**
A: Yes. 1099-NEC covers nonemployee compensation like freelance and contractor payments. 1099-MISC covers other payment types such as rent, royalties, and legal settlements. Using the wrong form can trigger IRS mismatch notices.

**Q: Do I need to file a 1099 for rent I pay a landlord?**
A: If you're a business paying $600 or more in rent as part of your trade or business, you generally need to issue a 1099-MISC to the landlord, unless the landlord is a corporation.
