## What the dependent care tax credit covers for summer camp costs

Westport parents juggling work schedules and July camp drop-offs often don't realize the IRS treats day camp as a work-related expense, not just a summer activity line item. The Child and Dependent Care Credit applies when you pay for care, including day camp, so you and your spouse (if filing jointly) can work or look for work. That means tuition for a sailing camp on the Sound, a coding camp in Fairfield, or a day program at the Y can count toward the credit, as long as the camp runs during daytime hours only.

Overnight camps don't qualify, no matter how educational or how expensive. The IRS draws a firm line here: care has to free you up to work, and overnight programs aren't structured that way. If your child splits time between a day camp and a part-time babysitter, both can count, provided total expenses stay within the caps described below.

## Who qualifies in Connecticut and what limits apply

The credit isn't unlimited, and eligibility depends on a few specifics that trip people up every filing season.

- Qualifying child: your dependent must be under age 13 when care was provided, or a spouse or dependent unable to care for themselves.
- Work requirement: you, and your spouse if married, need earned income from a job or self-employment during the period the camp expense was incurred.
- Expense caps: the IRS caps qualifying expenses at $3,000 for one dependent or $6,000 for two or more, regardless of actual cost.
- Credit percentage: depending on adjusted gross income, the credit runs between 20% and 35% of qualifying expenses, with most higher earners landing at the 20% floor.
- Connecticut layer: the state doesn't offer a separate dependent care credit, so the federal calculation is what matters, though it still flows through your [personal income tax planning](/services/personal-income-tax) each year.

## Dependent care FSA vs the tax credit for Westport families

Many clients in Fairfield County have access to an employer-sponsored dependent care FSA, and the natural question is whether to use that or claim the credit at tax time. You can't do both for the same dollars. An FSA lets a household set aside up to $5,000 pre-tax for dependent care, lowering taxable income immediately through payroll.

The tax credit, by contrast, is calculated after the fact on your return, using qualifying expenses not already covered by the FSA. For households earning above the mid-six-figure range, the FSA tends to deliver more value because it reduces income taxed at higher marginal rates. Lower-income households, or those without FSA access, often come out ahead with the credit alone. Some families use both: max out the FSA, then claim the credit on remaining expenses up to the IRS cap. Getting the split right takes a look at your specific income and camp costs as part of ongoing [tax planning](/services/tax), not a once-a-year guess.

## Records to keep for camp and childcare expenses

The credit only holds up if you can document it, and the IRS asks for specifics most parents don't think to save in the moment.

- Camp receipts or invoices showing dates of care, session cost, and the child's name.
- The camp or provider's tax identification number (EIN or SSN), needed to complete Form 2441.
- Proof of payment: canceled checks, credit card statements, or bank records tied to each session.
- A record of which weeks were day camp versus any overnight sessions, since only day camp qualifies.
- Earned income records for the same period, showing work or active job-hunting while the child was in care.

Keep these in one folder, digital or physical, as camp season happens. Recreating them in April is harder than it sounds.

## Common mistakes that reduce or disqualify the credit

The same mistakes surface every year, and most are avoidable with a second set of eyes before filing.

- Claiming overnight camp: sleepaway camps, even week-long ones, don't qualify under IRS rules, no exceptions.
- Missing provider ID: without the camp's EIN or SSN on Form 2441, the IRS can deny the entire claim.
- Care unrelated to work: if a stay-at-home parent had no earned income during camp weeks, those expenses generally don't qualify.
- Double-dipping with an FSA: claiming the same expenses through both a dependent care FSA and the credit triggers scrutiny and repayment.
- Overlooking relative caregivers: payments to a grandparent or other relative can qualify too, but only if properly reported and that person isn't your dependent.

## Frequently Asked Questions About Summer camps may qualify for dependent care tax credit

**Q: Does day camp qualify for the dependent care tax credit?**
A: Yes. The IRS treats day camp as a work-related expense when it allows a parent or both spouses to work or look for work. Overnight or sleepaway camps never qualify, regardless of educational content or cost, so the day-only rule matters when comparing camp options.

**Q: What is the maximum dependent care tax credit for 2024?**
A: Qualifying expenses are capped at $3,000 for one dependent or $6,000 for two or more. The credit itself runs between 20% and 35% of those expenses depending on adjusted gross income, with most higher-income households landing at the 20% rate.

**Q: Can I use a dependent care FSA and the tax credit together?**
A: You can, but not on the same dollars. Many Westport families max out an employer FSA up to $5,000 pre-tax, then claim the credit on any remaining qualifying expenses up to the IRS cap, which a CPA can calculate based on income.

**Q: Does Connecticut offer its own dependent care tax credit?**
A: No. Connecticut does not have a separate state-level dependent care credit. The federal Child and Dependent Care Credit is what applies, though it still affects your overall Connecticut tax picture through federal adjusted gross income.
